# Organized-crime violence and intergenerational persistence: evidence and bounds from Mexico

[ABSTRACT]
Mexico's homicide rate more than doubled after 2007. We ask whether the cohorts whose formative window, ages 10 to 18, fell inside that surge face higher intergenerational persistence of socioeconomic status. Using the ESRU-EMOVI 2023 retrospective mobility survey (15,558 non-migrant respondents born 1959-1998) together with state-level crime records for 1997-2017, we construct an exposure dose equal to the violence intensity of a respondent's state of residence at age 14 multiplied by the share of that respondent's formative window falling after 2007, and estimate a triple-difference on the rank-rank slope with state and birth-year fixed effects, each also interacted with origin rank. Unsupervised clustering of 21-year state violence trajectories, validated by silhouette, adjusted Rand index and subsample stability, serves both to test the common-calendar assumption embedded in the dose and to supply a partition of states built without reference to mobility. The estimated change in the slope is 0.016 (standard error 0.026) per standard deviation of intensity, 2.5 per cent of the base slope of 0.650; permutation inference over 2,000 reassignments of intensity across states yields p = 0.62, and the joint pre-trend test is not rejected (F = 0.42, p = 0.79). Recast as the absolute-mobility outcome used in the only direct precedent for this question, Sharkey and Torrats-Espinosa's (2017) study of United States counties, exposure lowers the expected destination rank of a child from the 25th percentile by 0.81 rank points (standard error 0.81), the same sign and about half the size of their instrumented estimate of 1.57, with a confidence interval that contains their value. Within-state municipal variation tightens the bound and excludes it. With 31 treated states against their 1,335 counties, the binding constraint is the number of treated units rather than the number of respondents, and we quantify that rather than conceal it.
[/ABSTRACT]

## 1 Introduction

Between 2007 and 2011 the homicide rate in Mexico roughly doubled, and the increase was not evenly spread: some states saw their rate triple within four years while others barely moved. A large literature now documents that children exposed to this violence accumulated less human capital, scored worse on standardised tests and left school earlier. What remains unknown is whether those losses translated into a change in the *transmission* of advantage across generations, which is a different question from whether they lowered average attainment. A shock can reduce everyone's schooling without altering the degree to which a person's position depends on the position of their parents. It can also leave averages untouched while tightening that dependence, if the families with resources absorb the shock and the families without resources do not.

This paper asks whether exposure to organized-crime violence during the formative window, taken here to be ages 10 to 18, raised intergenerational persistence in Mexico. The question matters for policy because the two margins call for different responses. If violence lowered attainment uniformly, the remedy is compensatory investment in the affected cohorts. If it raised persistence, the remedy has to be targeted at the bottom of the origin distribution, because that is where the additional damage concentrated.

Our measurement of mobility follows the rank-rank convention of Chetty et al. (2014): we rank respondents by an index of the assets of their household of origin, rank them again by an index of the assets of their household at the time of the survey, and take the slope of the second rank on the first. In our sample that slope is 0.650. The identification problem is that neither of the two comparisons a reader would reach for first is informative. Comparing states is uninformative because the northern states that became most violent were also, for reasons that predate the drug war, the most mobile; the cross-sectional correlation between violence intensity and persistence is -0.19 with a 95 per cent interval of [-0.51, +0.17], and its sign is the opposite of the hypothesis. Comparing cohorts is uninformative because the rank-rank slope was already rising between generations whose formative window closed in the 1990s, decades before there was anything for violence to explain.

The design we adopt requires the effect to appear in both dimensions at once. We assign each respondent a dose equal to the violence intensity of their state of residence at age 14, averaged over 2007-2017, multiplied by the share of their own window of ages 10 to 18 that fell after 2007. The coefficient of interest is that on the interaction of origin rank with the dose, in a specification that absorbs state fixed effects, birth-year fixed effects, and both of these interacted with origin rank. The last of those interactions is what prevents the pre-existing generational trend in the slope from being read as an effect of violence.

The data-science component of the paper addresses a specific weakness of that dose. Its cohort factor is mechanical: it presumes that the surge began in 2007 everywhere. That is an assumption, not a measurement. We therefore cluster the 32 state violence trajectories over 1997-2017, using Ward's hierarchical method, *k*-means and DBSCAN, on trajectories standardised within state so that shape is separated from level. The clustering is done without reference to any mobility outcome, which makes the resulting partition a legitimate source of heterogeneity rather than a search over subgroups. It recovers three regimes, among them a group of eleven states whose violence peaked in 2017 rather than 2010, for which the common 2007 calendar is demonstrably wrong.

Our central estimate is that one standard deviation of state violence intensity changes the rank-rank slope by 0.016, with a standard error of 0.026, clustered by state. That is 2.5 per cent of the base slope. Permutation inference, which does not rely on asymptotics in the number of clusters, gives p = 0.62. The joint test that the pre-surge cohorts show no differential slope is not rejected, with F = 0.42 and p = 0.79, so the design's identifying assumption survives the test available for it. Moving to within-state variation across 821 municipalities tightens the standard error, and the estimate there is 0.008 with a standard error of 0.018, of the same sign as the state estimate.

Recast in the units of the only direct precedent, the picture is more informative than a bare null. Sharkey and Torrats-Espinosa (2017) ask this question for United States counties and report, as their headline, the effect on the expected destination rank of a child from the 25th percentile. Our specification contains that quantity as a linear combination of coefficients we already estimate. In the state design it is -0.81 rank points per standard deviation of intensity, with a standard error of 0.81 — the same sign as their instrumented -1.57 and about half the magnitude — and the profile declines monotonically from the bottom of the origin distribution to the top, which is the pattern both the hypothesis and their results predict. Our confidence interval contains their estimate, so we cannot reject a United States-sized effect; in the within-state municipal design, whose interval is tighter, we can. The honest summary is that this design can bound the effect but not adjudicate it, and that the binding constraint is 31 treated states against their 1,335 counties rather than anything about the 15,558 respondents.

Three findings along the way are of independent interest. First, the crude cross-cohort comparison that motivates the hypothesis in the first place attributes to violence a trend that pre-dates it: moving from a specification without controls to the full specification cuts the coefficient from 0.047 to 0.016 once each birth cohort is allowed its own slope. Second, measuring municipal violence by homicide *counts* rather than rates produces a spurious negative and apparently significant coefficient, because within a state the municipalities with more homicides are the urban ones, which are more mobile for structural reasons; the municipal event study detects this, and the population denominator corrects it. Third, we find no detectable first stage at all: exposure moved neither years of schooling, nor the level of the destination asset index, nor the probability of inter-state migration — which parallels Sharkey and Torrats-Espinosa's own finding of no effect on college attendance.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 situates the contribution. Section 3 describes the context, the data and the construction of the variables. Section 4 sets out the empirical strategy, including the clustering procedure and its validation. Section 5 reports results. Section 6 discusses implications and limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Related literature

### 2.1 Violence and human capital

The effect of violence on the human capital of the young is by now well documented, and the Mexican surge has been one of its main laboratories. Brown and Velásquez (2017) find that young adults exposed to the increase in homicides reduced schooling and labour supply. Michaelsen and Salardi (2020) show that violence near schools lowered test scores, and attribute the effect to psychological stress rather than to school closure or displacement. Jarillo et al. (2016) document effects on both students and teachers. Velásquez (2020) estimates the broader economic burden of crime in Mexico, including occupational choice and the labour supply of the self-employed. Outside Mexico, the finding replicates: Monteiro and Rocha (2017) for drug-gang conflict in Rio de Janeiro, Foureaux Koppensteiner and Menezes (2021) for homicides near Brazilian schools, Shemyakina (2011) for Tajikistan and León (2012) for Peru all report reductions in schooling attributable to exposure during childhood or adolescence.

One feature of this literature matters for what follows: its outcome is almost always a *level* — years of schooling, test scores, enrolment, earnings — rather than the gradient linking a person's position to their parents'. Brown and Velásquez are explicit that their average effect on completed schooling is not statistically significant and that the action is in the heterogeneity, which is itself a hint that the distributional margin may be where the damage sits.

### 2.2 Violence and mobility: the direct precedent

The question we ask has been asked once, for the United States. Sharkey and Torrats-Espinosa (2017) estimate the effect of violent crime experienced between ages 14 and 17 on the economic mobility of the cohorts born 1980-1986, using the county-level mobility measures of Chetty et al. (2014) across roughly 1,335 counties and 8,867 county-cohort cells. Their design is a two-way fixed-effect difference-in-differences, instrumented with federal COPS police-hiring grants. Their headline outcome is *absolute upward mobility*, the expected income rank at age 26 of a child whose parents sat at the 25th percentile; the rank-rank slope appears only as a secondary outcome they label relative immobility. They find that a one standard deviation increase in the county murder rate lowers the expected rank at the 25th percentile by about 1.57 points, with effects that weaken and lose significance for children from the 75th percentile upwards. They also report no detectable effect on college attendance, with only suggestive evidence on high-school dropout.

Mann et al. (2024) run the arrow the other way, asking whether low mobility predicts later community violence, and find that it does, more strongly than poverty, inequality or unemployment. Taken together these two papers establish that the association exists in the United States and that its direction is not obvious a priori.

Our contribution is therefore not the question but the setting, the design and the parameter. Mexico differs from the United States in that the shock is far larger and far more concentrated in time, which is an advantage; and in that the available mobility data are a retrospective survey with 31 treated states rather than an administrative panel of 1,335 counties, which is a severe disadvantage for precision and which we quantify rather than conceal. We make the rank-rank slope the primary parameter and report the absolute-mobility profile alongside it, in their units, so the two papers can be read against each other. Section 5.8 does that comparison explicitly.

### 2.3 Intergenerational mobility in Mexico

The measurement of mobility in Mexico rests on the ESRU-EMOVI surveys conducted by the Centro de Estudios Espinosa Yglesias, which collect retrospective information on the household of origin at age 14 and current circumstances for a nationally representative sample of adults. The headline findings are of high persistence at both tails and of substantial regional heterogeneity: Delajara et al. (2022) show that the probability of escaping the bottom quintile differs markedly across Mexican regions, with the south far less mobile than the north. The CEEY's own reports (CEEY 2019) document the same regional gradient and frame it as a question of regional equality of opportunity. Torche (2014) places the Mexican case within the comparative Latin American evidence, and emphasises that high inequality and high persistence coexist in ways that complicate the usual cross-country inference.

That regional gradient is central to our identification problem rather than to our answer. Because the states that became most violent after 2007 are concentrated in the north, and the north was already the more mobile part of the country, any cross-sectional comparison of violence and persistence recovers the pre-existing geography rather than the effect of the surge. The design in Section 4 is built to remove it.

The life-cycle literature provides a further caution. Haider and Solon (2006) and Nybom and Stuhler (2016) show that persistence estimated from snapshots taken at different ages is biased in a direction that depends on where in the life cycle each cohort is observed. Because our younger cohorts are also our exposed cohorts, this is a first-order concern. We return to it in Section 5.3, where we show that the observed cross-cohort gradient has the wrong sign for life-cycle bias to explain it.

### 2.4 Data-science methods in mobility and inequality research

Computational methods have entered this field along two routes. The first is prediction in the service of measurement. Bloise et al. (2021) use supervised learning to impute parental income in two-stage estimates of intergenerational mobility, selecting among first-stage specifications by out-of-sample prediction error and thereby replacing a discretionary choice with an objective criterion. The second is the estimation of inequality of opportunity, where Brunori et al. (2023) use regression trees and forests to partition circumstances without imposing the analyst's own cut-offs, and Salas-Rojo and Rodríguez (2022) use random forests to choose the thresholds that discretise a continuous circumstance, showing that conventional discretionary cut-offs yield unstable results.

In the journal to which this paper is addressed, the two reference points for how such methods are reported are Lechner and Okasa (2025), who develop a random-forest estimator of the ordered choice model with honest sample splitting and inference, assessing performance by mean squared error and the ranked probability score under repeated cross-validation; and Caravaggio et al. (2026), who compare Elastic-Net, random forest, gradient boosting and support-vector regression against a multivariate regression baseline, tune hyperparameters by repeated ten-fold cross-validation on the training split alone, average over one hundred random 80/20 partitions, and interpret the fitted models through standardised variable importance and partial dependence.

Our use of these methods differs in kind, and we are explicit about it. The clustering in Section 4.3 is unsupervised: there is no label to predict, and therefore no out-of-sample prediction error to report. What there is instead is a hyperparameter, the number of clusters, chosen by an internal criterion; a question of algorithmic robustness, answered by comparing partitions across methods; and a question of stability, answered by repeated estimation on random subsamples. We report all three, and we state what each can and cannot establish. The methodological precedent we follow most closely is the use of unsupervised partitions as a *diagnostic* for an assumption embedded in a parametric specification, rather than as a predictive model in its own right.

## 3 Context and data

### 3.1 The violence surge

The proximate cause of the Mexican surge is the federal confrontation with trafficking organisations that began in December 2006, and the mechanisms by which it produced violence are now reasonably well established: enforcement that removed organisational leadership triggered succession struggles and territorial competition (Calderón et al. 2015), and the resulting conflict concentrated along trafficking routes and in municipalities contested by more than one organisation (Dell 2015). For our purposes the relevant implication is that the geographic incidence of the surge was determined by the pre-existing logistics of the drug trade rather than by the socioeconomic characteristics of the states, which is what makes a design built on differential state intensity plausible in the first place.

Figure 1 shows the national trajectory of the four crimes we use. Intentional homicide, in panel (a), is flat or declining through 2007 and then rises steeply, peaking around 2011. Panel (b) shows extortion, kidnapping and violent highway robbery, the crimes of economic predation that require an organisation capable of sustaining a territorial threat. They follow the same break, which is why we treat them as manifestations of a single phenomenon rather than as separate outcomes. We deliberately exclude ordinary theft, which is a crime of opportunity and does not require such an organisation; including it would dilute the attribution to organized crime.

### 3.2 The ESRU-EMOVI 2023 survey

Our mobility data come from the ESRU-EMOVI 2023, a nationally representative retrospective survey of adults aged 25 to 64 conducted by the Centro de Estudios Espinosa Yglesias. The survey asks each respondent about the characteristics of the household in which they lived at age 14, including services, dwelling characteristics, durable goods and property, and about their current household along the same dimensions. It also records the state and municipality of residence at age 14, which is what allows us to attach a violence history to each respondent.

Two features of the instrument shape the analysis. First, the survey records assets rather than income, both at origin and at destination. This is a strength for the origin measure, since recalled assets are more reliably reported than recalled income, and a limitation for the destination measure, since assets accumulate over the life cycle and therefore depend on the age at which a respondent is observed. Second, the survey is a repeated cross-section rather than a panel: we observe each respondent once.

### 3.3 Measuring exposure

Our violence data are the SESNSP state-level series of reported crime under the earlier methodology, covering 1997-2017, which is the longest consistent series available at state level and the only one with a pre-surge period long enough to validate the design. For each state and year we compute rates per 100,000 inhabitants of the four crimes, standardise each across the full panel, and average the four standardised rates into a violence index. The intensity of a state, I(e), is the mean of that index over 2007-2017.

The unit of record deserves a sentence, because it is not what a reader may assume. The SESNSP counts *investigation files opened* — averiguaciones previas under the former inquisitorial system and carpetas de investigación under the accusatorial one that states adopted at different dates between 2011 and 2016. It does not count victims, and it does not count crimes: one file may cover several victims, and a crime that generates no complaint generates no file.

Two consequences follow, and both cut against us rather than for us. The first is level under-count. Mexico's victimisation survey puts the share of crimes that never reach a public prosecutor very high, so the series measures recorded crime, not crime. For intentional homicide the gap is far smaller than for other offences, which is one reason the index is anchored on it. The second consequence matters more for identification. Comparing the SESNSP series with INEGI's vital-statistics count of homicide deaths, which is built from death certificates rather than prosecutors' files, the two disagree by roughly seven per cent nationally, and the disagreement varies across states and changes sign in some of them over time. That is non-classical measurement error in the treatment, potentially correlated with it.

We cannot sign the resulting bias without the vital-statistics series. What we can say is that classical attenuation would push our estimate towards zero, so our bound is conservative under the classical case, and that replicating the exposure index on INEGI death records — which also begin in 1990 rather than 1997, and so would extend the pre-period by seven years — is the single most valuable extension to this design. We flag it as the first item of unfinished business rather than as a solved problem.

The cohort component is the share of a respondent's formative window that fell inside the surge:

[EQ]φ(c)  =  #{ y ∈ [c+10, c+18] : y ≥ 2007 }  ⁄  9[/EQ]

where c is the birth year. By construction φ(c) = 0 for cohorts born before 1989 and φ(c) = 1 for cohorts born from 1997 onwards, with a linear ramp between. Figure 2 shows the two components separately: panel (a) the cohort ramp, panel (b) the distribution of intensity across states.

[FIGURE:Fig2|The two components of the exposure dose. Panel (a) plots the share of the formative window, ages 10 to 18, falling after 2007, by birth year. Panel (b) plots the mean violence index over 2007-2017 by state, sorted; hatched bars denote states with no observations in the estimation sample. State abbreviations follow INEGI codes.]

The dose assigned to a respondent born in year c and resident in state e at age 14 is

[EQ]D(e,c)  =  I(e)  ×  φ(c)[/EQ]

Throughout we report coefficients scaled by the standard deviation of I(e) across states, which is 0.409. We do this because one unit of raw dose corresponds to moving from never exposed to the most violent state for the entire adolescence, a change so large that coefficients expressed per unit of dose are not interpretable. The two scales differ by a factor of 2.4 and must not be mixed.

### 3.4 Variables, missing data and survey design

Both asset indices are the first principal component of the relevant survey items, computed by singular value decomposition. The origin index uses 42 binary items referring to age 14, covering dwelling services, dwelling characteristics, durable goods and property, plus one count item; the destination index uses 39 binary items referring to the present, plus one count item. Binary items are coded one for present, and the responses "does not know" and "does not answer" are set to missing and mean-imputed before the decomposition; the count items are binarised at a positive value. The sign of each component is oriented so that higher values denote more resources. The first component explains 21.0 per cent of the variance of the origin block and 16.1 per cent of the destination block, which is typical for asset indices of this kind (Filmer and Pritchett 2001; Vyas and Kumaranayake 2006).

Ranks are computed within survey-defined cohort, using the expansion factors, as [cumsum(w) − ½·w] ⁄ Σw over the ordering of the index. Computing ranks within cohort rather than over the pooled sample is what makes the rank-rank slope comparable across cohorts that face different asset price levels and different stages of accumulation.

Years of schooling are obtained by mapping the survey's thirteen-category schooling variable to completed years, with the category "none" mapped to zero. We flag this because an earlier version of our own analysis truncated the scale by dropping the highest categories along with the code for "none", which produced two spurious significant coefficients that vanished on correction.

Rural origin is defined as residence at age 14 in a locality of fewer than 2,500 inhabitants, which is INEGI's definition and corresponds to a single code in the survey; a broader coding that included localities of up to 99,999 inhabitants classified 68 per cent of the sample as rural and is not used.

All estimates use the survey expansion factors. Standard errors are clustered at the level of the treatment, which is the state in the main design and the municipality in the within-state design. The survey's primary sampling units and strata are not used for variance estimation because the treatment is assigned at a coarser level than the sampling clusters, so clustering on the treatment is the binding requirement.

### 3.5 The analytical sample

We restrict the sample to respondents who were living in one of the 32 states at age 14 and who had not moved to a different state by the time of the survey. The restriction is necessary because the dose is defined by the state of the formative window, and respondents who moved have a destination determined partly by a labour market we do not assign to them. It is also a limitation, since migration is not random: Aldeco Leo et al. (2024) show that Mexican internal migration did respond to the homicide surge. Two things mitigate it. Their displacement is mostly short-distance, within the same commuting zone, which means it is largely *within* state and so survives our restriction, which is on inter-state moves only. And we show in Section 5.7 that the dose does not detectably predict inter-state migration, with a coefficient of 0.008 and an upper 95 per cent limit of 0.035. We also report the estimate including migrants as a robustness check.

The resulting sample has 15,558 respondents born between 1959 and 1998, resident in 31 states (Tlaxcala has no observations satisfying the restriction) and, for the municipal design, 821 municipalities of origin. Of these respondents, 5,035, or 32.4 per cent, have a positive dose.

The exposed and unexposed groups are nearly identical in origin rank, destination rank, violence intensity and sex, which is what the design requires of the state component: exposure status is driven by birth year, not by the characteristics of the state. The two groups differ, as expected, in schooling, which rose across cohorts, and in co-residence with parents, which is mechanically higher for younger respondents. We address the latter by reporting an estimate that excludes co-residents.

## 4 Methodology

### 4.1 Why the simpler comparisons do not work

We take as the parameter of interest the rank-rank slope β₁ in rank_des = β₀ + β₁·rank_ori + ε, and we ask whether it shifts with exposure. Two simpler strategies suggest themselves and both fail.

The first compares states. Estimating the slope separately within each state and correlating it with violence intensity over the 31 states in the sample yields -0.19, with a 95 per cent interval of [-0.51, +0.17]. The correlation is weak, imprecise and of the wrong sign, and it is also fragile: dropping the single state with fewer than one hundred observations moves it to -0.34, with an interval of [-0.63, +0.02]. One observation should not move a national correlation that far. The substantive reason it is uninformative is the regional geography documented by Delajara et al. (2022): the northern states were both more violent and more mobile before the surge began.

The second compares cohorts. The slope rises monotonically across birth-year bands: 0.554 for those born in 1959-1966, 0.600 for 1967-1974, 0.665 for 1975-1984, 0.681 for 1985-1991 and 0.693 for 1992-1998. The temptation is to read the last two figures as the effect of the surge. But the first three bands contain no exposed respondents at all — every one of them completed the formative window before 2007 — and the slope had already risen by 0.111 across them, which is more than the remaining increase. Whatever drives that trend, it is not the drug war, and attributing it to violence would be an error.

The design therefore has to require that the effect appear in both dimensions simultaneously, which is what a triple difference does.

### 4.2 The triple-difference specification

We estimate

[EQ]rank_des(i)  =  β₁·rank_ori(i)  +  β₂·D(e,c)  +  β₃·[rank_ori(i) × D(e,c)][/EQ][EQ]              +  α(e)  +  γ(c)  +  [α(e) × rank_ori(i)]  +  [γ(c) × rank_ori(i)]  +  ε(i)[/EQ]

by weighted least squares with the survey expansion factors, where α(e) and γ(c) are state and birth-year fixed effects. The parameter of interest is β₃. Writing the specification as

[EQ]rank_des(i)  =  [ β₁  +  β₃·D(e,c) ] · rank_ori(i)  +  ⋯[/EQ]

makes its meaning explicit: the rank-rank slope is no longer a constant but a function of the dose, and β₃ is the change in that slope per unit of dose.

The four fixed-effect blocks each remove a specific confound. State fixed effects absorb permanent differences in the *level* of destination rank; birth-year fixed effects absorb cohort-wide changes in that level. The interactions are the ones that matter for identification. State-by-origin-rank effects absorb permanent differences in *persistence* across states, which is what defeats the cross-sectional comparison. Birth-year-by-origin-rank effects absorb the generational trend in persistence, which is what defeats the cross-cohort comparison. Omitting the last block is not innocuous: as Section 5.2 shows, it inflates β₃ by a factor of three, and it also fabricates a pre-trend in the event study that disappears once the block is included.

The identifying assumption is that, absent the surge, the relationship between origin rank and destination rank would have evolved in parallel across states with different intensities. This is a parallel-trends assumption on a slope rather than on a level, and the pre-surge cohorts provide a direct test of it, which we report in Section 5.3.

### 4.3 The data-science component: clustering violence trajectories

The cohort factor φ(c) imposes a single calendar on the whole country: it assumes the surge began in 2007 in every state. If some states turned violent later, their respondents are assigned a dose that is mis-dated, and mis-dating attenuates β₃ towards zero. Whether the common calendar holds is an empirical question that can be answered without looking at mobility at all, which is the reason for the unsupervised exercise.

**Feature construction.** For each of the 32 states we take the 21 annual values of the violence index and standardise them within the state, subtracting the state's own mean and dividing by its own standard deviation. This separates the *shape* of a trajectory from its *level*: two states with the same temporal profile cluster together even if one is far more violent than the other. The resulting feature matrix is 32 by 21.

**Algorithms.** We apply three algorithms that differ in what they assume. Ward's hierarchical agglomeration (Ward 1963) produces a full dendrogram and does not require fixing the number of clusters in advance. *k*-means is partitional and does require it, which makes the two a useful cross-check. DBSCAN (Ester et al. 1996) is density-based and can decline to assign points at all, labelling them noise; we apply it both to states and to municipalities precisely to show in which sample-size regime it is informative.

**Hyperparameter selection.** The number of clusters is the hyperparameter. We select it by mean silhouette (Rousseeuw 1987), computed for k = 2, …, 8 for both Ward and *k*-means. For DBSCAN the hyperparameters are the neighbourhood radius and the minimum number of points; we report results over a grid of radii rather than selecting one, because at the state level no radius produces an informative partition.

**Validation.** An unsupervised problem has no held-out label, so there is no prediction error to report and we do not report one. We validate along three axes instead. *Internal cohesion*: the cophenetic correlation between the dendrogram's implied distances and the true pairwise distances, which measures how faithfully the hierarchy represents the data. *Cross-algorithm agreement*: the adjusted Rand index (Hubert and Arabie 1985) between the Ward and *k*-means partitions at the selected k, which tests whether the structure is a property of the data or of one algorithm's assumptions. *Stability under resampling*: we draw 500 random subsamples of 80 per cent of the states, re-run Ward's method on each, and compute the adjusted Rand index between the resulting partition and the full-sample partition restricted to the drawn states. This is the sample-splitting analogue available to an unsupervised problem, and it is the statistic we would want a reader to judge the partition by.

**The benchmark.** The relevant comparison model is the one the dose already embodies: a single regime with a common 2007 break, which is the k = 1 case. The clustering is informative to the extent that it rejects that restriction, and Section 5.4 reports the sense in which it does.

**Use of the result.** The partition enters the paper twice. It is a diagnostic for the dose, and it is a partition of the country constructed without reference to the outcome, which makes estimating the model within each cluster a legitimate heterogeneity exercise rather than a search over subgroups. We stress that the clusters are not themselves a treatment and that no causal claim attaches to them.

### 4.4 Inference with few clusters

With 31 states, cluster-robust standard errors rest on an asymptotic approximation in the number of clusters that is known to be unreliable at this count, and to over-reject (Bertrand et al. 2004; Cameron and Miller 2015). We therefore complement them with a randomisation test in the spirit of Young (2019). Holding the panel of respondents fixed, we permute the vector of state intensities across states, recompute β₃, and repeat 2,000 times. The reported p-value is the share of permutations whose |β₃| exceeds the observed value. To make this feasible we residualise by the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell theorem, taking a QR decomposition of the fixed-effect block once and residualising only the two columns that change with each permutation. Within clusters, where the count falls to between 6 and 15 states, we rely on the permutation result rather than on the cluster-robust statistic.

### 4.5 Within-state municipal variation

Measuring violence at the state level averages away most of the variation that respondents actually experienced: the within-state standard deviation of municipal violence is 0.71 against a total of 1.00, so roughly half of the dispersion is internal to states. Using municipal variation therefore raises precision. It also permits a stronger specification in principle, since treatment that varies within a state admits state-by-cohort fixed effects that would absorb any shock common to a state at a point in time. In practice that specification is not estimable at our sample size, and we say so in Section 5.6 rather than reporting an unstable version of it.

We define municipal intensity as the log of one plus the annual homicide rate per 100,000, using SESNSP municipal homicide counts for 2011-2017 and CONAPO mid-year municipal population projections averaged over the same period, standardised across municipalities. The within-state deviation is that quantity minus its state mean, and the municipal dose is the deviation times φ(c). Two limitations follow from the data. The municipal series begins in 2011, so the pre-surge window is much shorter than at state level and the pre-trend test is correspondingly weaker. And the choice of denominator turns out to be decisive rather than cosmetic, for reasons we document in Section 5.6.

### 4.6 What the design can and cannot identify

The estimate of β₃ is a causal quantity under the parallel-trends assumption stated in Section 4.2, and that assumption is testable on the pre-surge cohorts. Three qualifications bound the claim.

First, the design cannot separate violence from anything else that varied by state and by cohort during the exposure window. We note first what is *not* a threat, because an earlier version of this paper reported it as one. The COVID-19 pandemic, the 2019 termination of the PROSPERA conditional transfer programme and the 2022 end of the full-time schools programme are sometimes raised as confounds for work on these cohorts. They are not confounds here, on timing: the oldest respondent in our sample was born in 1959 and the youngest in 1998, so the latest formative window in the data closes in **2016**, and not one respondent — zero of 15,558 — had a window still open when any of those three shocks arrived. The youngest person in the sample was 22 when the pandemic began. Those events can affect the destination assets we measure in 2023, but they arrive simultaneously for every cohort and are therefore absorbed rather than confounding.

What does have the right timing is the 2008-09 recession. It fell squarely inside the exposure window, and it fell hardest on the manufacturing states of the north, which are also where violence rose most. This is the confound we cannot dismiss on timing and can only partly address: state fixed effects absorb its permanent component and birth-year fixed effects its national component, but a state-specific recession shock that coincided with the violence surge would not be separated by our design. Controlling for state economic activity interacted with cohort is the natural next step and requires state product or employment series we do not use here.

Second, the sample is of survivors who stayed. People who died, disappeared or emigrated are not in the survey, and they did not leave at random. If violence removed the least advantaged from the sample, our estimate understates persistence among the exposed.

Third, the clustering is descriptive. It characterises the timing of violence and nothing more.

### 5.1 Main estimates

Methodologically, the path is not monotone, which is precisely why the specification must be fixed before the results are inspected rather than chosen from among them.

### 5.3 Testing the identifying assumption

[FIGURE:Fig3|Event study by birth-year group. Each point is the coefficient on the interaction of origin rank with state violence intensity for one birth-year group, relative to the 1979-82 group, expressed per standard deviation of intensity, with 95 per cent confidence intervals. We replace the dose with interactions of origin rank and violence intensity with each birth-year group, taking 1979-82 as reference. Groups whose formative window closed before 2007 have a dose that is mechanically zero, so a non-zero coefficient for them is a differential pre-trend and would invalidate the design.

Panel (a) of Figure 3 shows the state design. The four pre-surge groups are individually indistinguishable from zero and the joint test does not reject, with F = 0.42 and p = 0.79. We note that an earlier version of this specification, which omitted the birth-year-by-origin-rank block, rejected the joint test at p = 0.039; the pre-trend it detected was the life-cycle and generational trend that the omitted block absorbs, not a failure of the design.

This is also where the life-cycle concern of Haider and Solon (2006) is addressed. Life-cycle bias predicts *lower* measured persistence among respondents observed earlier in their accumulation, that is among the young, and therefore among the exposed. The observed cross-cohort gradient in Section 4.1 runs the other way. A second check points the same way. Harmonising the 2017 and 2023 rounds of the ESRU-EMOVI onto 26 common origin items and 23 common destination items, and comparing the same birth cohorts across rounds, the slope for those born 1959-1991 is 0.637 when they are observed in 2017 and 0.583 when the same cohorts are observed six years older in 2023, a difference of -0.053; the same ordering holds in each band separately, at -0.089, -0.043 and -0.023. Observing people later in the life cycle lowers measured persistence here, which is again the opposite of what life-cycle attenuation predicts. The comparison confounds age with survey period and we do not press it further. With two rounds, age, period and cohort effects cannot be separately identified, so we do not claim to know what generates the gradient; we claim only that it pre-dates the surge and that the specification absorbs it.

Mean silhouette is maximised at k = 3 for both algorithms, at 0.236 for Ward and 0.247 for *k*-means, and the two partitions agree closely at that value, with an adjusted Rand index of 0.849. The cophenetic correlation of the Ward hierarchy is 0.603. Under resampling the partition is stable: over 500 draws of 80 per cent of the states the mean adjusted Rand index against the full-sample partition is 0.865, with a fifth percentile of 0.631. DBSCAN, by contrast, returns a single cluster at every radius we try, sending between 3 and 14 of the 32 states to noise. At 32 observations the density is too sparse for a density-based method, and we report this as a negative result about the algorithm rather than about the data: applied to the 533 municipalities with at least 35 homicides over 2011-2017, the same algorithm separates a bulk from a tail of atypical trajectories, which is what it is for.

Measured against the benchmark of Section 4.3, the single-regime model that the dose assumes, the three-cluster partition accounts for 38.8 per cent of the total sum of squares in trajectory shape. We report two further internal criteria because they do not agree with the silhouette, and the disagreement is worth seeing. The Calinski-Harabasz index is maximised at k = 2, at 11.46 against 9.18 at k = 3, and declines thereafter; the Davies-Bouldin index keeps improving as k rises, reaching its minimum at the largest value we examine. Criteria of this kind routinely disagree, and with 32 observations none of them is decisive. What makes the result usable is that the substantive conclusion does not depend on which one is followed. The first-wave cluster of fifteen states is identical at k = 2, k = 3 and k = 4, and all eleven late-peaking states remain together in a single group at each of those values. The claim we take from the exercise — that a distinct group of states experienced its surge roughly a decade after the date the dose assigns — is therefore invariant to the hyperparameter over the range the criteria dispute.

The three regimes are substantively interpretable, and panel (c) of Figure 4 shows them. Fifteen states, including Chihuahua, Sinaloa, Michoacán and Baja California, peak around 2010 and then recede; these are the states of the first wave. Eleven states, including Guerrero, Veracruz, Zacatecas and Colima, are flat through 2010 and rise to their maximum in 2017; for them the common 2007 calendar is wrong, and the surge they experienced is largely outside the window our dose measures. Six states peak in 1997, at the start of the series, and have below-average intensity thereafter.

The eleven late-peaking states are the substantive finding of the exercise. Their respondents are assigned a dose dated to a surge that had not yet reached them, which attenuates β₃. This is a Berkson-type problem in the timing of the treatment rather than classical measurement error in its level, and re-dating the dose with each cluster's own calendar is the most direct improvement available to this design.

Panel C of Table 4 reports the model within each cluster. The cluster-robust statistics look striking — 0.085 with a *t* of 2.59 in the first-wave cluster, -0.035 with a *t* of -2.64 in the late cluster — and we do not believe them. With between 6 and 15 states per cluster the cluster-robust variance is biased downward, and the permutation test within each cluster returns p-values of 0.19, 0.38 and 0.79. We report both columns so that the gap between them is visible, because that gap is the practical content of the few-clusters problem.

### 5.5 Re-dating the dose with each cluster's calendar

The previous section identifies a defect in our own exposure measure. The obvious response is to fix it, and we do, with the result that the fix costs more than it buys.

We estimate rather than impose the start of each surge. For each cluster we fit a level shift to the mean standardised trajectory, searching over candidate break years from 2001 to 2015 and choosing the one that minimises the residual sum of squares. The estimated breaks are 2008 for the first-wave cluster, 2011 for the late cluster and 2010 for the low-intensity cluster, with shift magnitudes of 1.26, 1.50 and 0.56 within-state standard deviations. We then redefine both components of the dose coherently: the timing comes from the standardised series, because shape is what dates a surge, while the intensity is recomputed as the mean of the *unstandardised* index over each state's own post-break window, because level is what measures how violent a state became. Using the standardised series for both would erase precisely the cross-state level differences the dose needs. We repeat the exercise with a break estimated separately for each state, falling back to the cluster break for the six states whose best-fitting break is a decline rather than a surge.

[TABLE:t7|Estimates under three datings of the exposure dose. Row (1) imposes a common 2007 start; rows (2) and (3) estimate the start by structural break at the cluster and the state level respectively. Coefficients are per standard deviation of the corresponding intensity measure; standard errors clustered by state in parentheses.]

The point estimate roughly doubles, from 0.016 under the common calendar to 0.034 under cluster re-dating and 0.028 under state re-dating. That is the direction attenuation predicts, and to that extent the mis-dating diagnosis in Section 5.4 is vindicated. But the standard error grows faster than the coefficient, from 0.026 to 0.036 and 0.042, so the minimum detectable effect deteriorates from 11 per cent of the base slope to 16 and 18 per cent, and the estimate remains indistinguishable from zero throughout. The mechanism is mechanical: later start dates push cohorts out of exposure, and the share of the sample with any exposure falls from 32.4 to 26.7 per cent. Correcting the dating trades treated observations for cleaner treatment, and at our sample size the trade is unfavourable.

Two things are worth taking from this. Substantively, the attenuation was real but modest relative to the noise: re-dating does not reveal a hidden effect. Methodologically, we report the common-2007 specification as primary because it was fixed before any of this was estimated and because it maximises power, not because it gives a smaller number — it gives a smaller number, and we say so. The re-dated event study also passes its pre-trend test more comfortably than the original, with F = 0.37 and p = 0.83.

### 5.6 The within-state municipal design

[TABLE:t4|Estimates using within-state municipal variation. The treatment is the municipality's deviation from its state mean, interacted with the share of the formative window after 2007. Table 6 reports the municipal results, and the order of the columns is the point. Column (1) measures municipal violence by the log count of homicides, as the raw SESNSP series provides it. The estimate is -0.0344 with a standard error of 0.0232. Taken at face value this is a sizeable negative effect, and it is the result we initially obtained.

It is an artefact of the denominator. Within a state, the municipalities with more homicides are the larger and more urban ones, and urban origin predicts higher mobility for reasons that have nothing to do with violence. The within-state correlation between the log count and rural origin is -0.30, and -0.37 in levels. The municipal event study detects the problem: with counts, the pre-surge groups show a differential trend that the design cannot tolerate.

Column (2) replaces counts with the homicide rate per 100,000, using CONAPO municipal population projections. The correlation with rural origin falls to -0.11, the negative estimate disappears, and β₃ becomes 0.0084 with a standard error of 0.0176 — the same sign as the state estimate and more precise. Adding locality-size controls interacted with origin rank, in column (3), leaves it at 0.0076. Clustering by state rather than by municipality, in column (4), gives the same point estimate with a standard error of 0.0183, so the inference is not sensitive to the clustering level.

Panel (b) of Figure 3 shows the municipal event study with the rate. The joint pre-trend test is not rejected, with p = 0.13 in the specification with locality-size controls and p = 0.22 without them. We report this as weaker evidence than the state design provides, and deliberately so: all four pre-surge coefficients are negative, and two have *t*-statistics near -1.7, so there is a mild drift that the test does not reject but does not rule out either. Because the municipal series begins in 2011, the pre-period available here is short. For these reasons we treat the state design as the primary specification and the municipal design as a refinement of its bound.

We also attempted the state-by-cohort fixed-effect specification that within-state variation makes conceptually possible. It is not estimable at our sample size: the design matrix is singular with the full interaction, and coarsening the cohort bands enough to restore rank inflates the standard error beyond usefulness. We note, however, that the concern it would address is partly self-limiting, because the within-state deviation averages to zero within each state by construction.

### 5.8 The absolute-mobility profile and the United States benchmark

Our specification contains more than the slope. Because the destination rank is modelled as (β₁ + β₃·D)·p + β₂·D + …, the effect of the dose on the expected destination rank of someone from origin percentile p is β₂ + p·β₃, a linear combination of coefficients we have already estimated. That quantity is the outcome Sharkey and Torrats-Espinosa make primary, and reporting it puts the two papers on one scale. We express it in rank points out of 100, as they do.

[TABLE:t8|Effect of exposure on the expected destination rank, by origin percentile. Entries are rank points out of 100 per standard deviation of violence intensity, computed as the linear combination β₂ + p·β₃ with standard errors from the same clustered covariance matrix. The state design clusters by state, the within-state municipal design by municipality.]

In the state design the profile is negative at the bottom and flat at the top: -1.05 points at the 10th percentile, -0.81 at the 25th, -0.41 at the median, and statistically indistinguishable from zero at the 75th and 90th. The sign pattern is the one the hypothesis predicts and the one Sharkey and Torrats-Espinosa report, including their finding that effects fade above the 75th percentile. None of the individual estimates is significant.

The comparison with their magnitude is the useful part, and it cuts two ways. Our state-design estimate at the 25th percentile, -0.81 with a standard error of 0.81, has the same sign as their instrumented estimate of -1.57 and about half the size, and our 95 per cent interval of [-2.40, +0.78] **contains** their value. We therefore cannot reject an effect as large as the one found for the United States; our minimum detectable effect at that percentile, 2.27 rank points, is larger than their estimate. In the within-state municipal design the interval at the 25th percentile is [-1.14, +1.87], which **excludes** -1.57: there, and only there, can we rule out a United States-sized effect.

Three caveats keep that from being a clean rejection, and we would rather state them than have a referee supply them. Their standard deviation is of a county murder rate and ours of a within-state municipal deviation, so one standard deviation is not the same object. Their outcome is an income rank from administrative records and ours an asset rank from a retrospective survey. And the municipal design is the one whose pre-trend test is weaker. What the comparison does establish is the scale of the question: with 31 treated states we are not in a position to adjudicate an effect of the size the United States literature reports, and with 821 municipalities we barely are.

Finally, adopting their exposure window rather than ours changes little. Defining the dose over ages 14 to 17 instead of 10 to 18 gives an effect at the 25th percentile of -0.72 points with a standard error of 0.74, and a slope coefficient of 0.0155 against our 0.0161. The result is not an artefact of where we put the window.

### 5.9 What can and cannot be ruled out

The minimum detectable effect at 80 per cent power and 5 per cent significance is 2.8 times the standard error, where 2.8 = 1.96 + 0.84. In the state design this is 0.0715, or 11.0 per cent of the base slope. In the municipal design it is 7.6 per cent, which we round to 8 per cent. These are the numbers we regard as the result of the paper.

The honest statement is therefore not that violence does not affect mobility. It is that exposure during the formative window does not change the rank-rank slope by more than 8 per cent of its base value, and that an effect smaller than that would be invisible to this design. We also report what the alternative routes to greater power delivered, since all of them failed and the failures are informative. Quintile transitions, which target the hypothesis more sharply, require 25 per cent of the base slope in the same units because conditioning on the bottom quintile leaves only 2,236 observations; they lose more in sample than they gain in sharpness. Pooling the ESRU-EMOVI 2017 round with 2023, which required harmonising 26 origin and 23 destination items, raised the minimum detectable effect from 11 to 12 per cent rather than lowering it, because the 2017 round is almost entirely composed of controls: only 14 per cent of its respondents have any exposure at all, and the maximum value of φ there is 0.44, so no respondent in that round spent even half of the formative window inside the surge.

### 5.10 Rebuilding the exposure index on vital statistics

From INEGI mortality microdata we rebuild the exposure index on homicide *deaths* by state and year of occurrence from 1990 to 2020, with CONAPO mid-year population as the denominator. The two sources correlate at 0.903 over 672 state-by-year cells and agree within 2 per cent in the aggregate, but state by state the gap runs from −54 per cent in Tlaxcala to +34 per cent in Chihuahua: the measurement error is not a constant factor.

The longer series tests an assumption the design had so far asserted. Our pre-surge cohorts are treated as unexposed, yet the national homicide rate between 1990 and 1997 was 13.6 per 100,000 against 19.7 during the surge, and 38.9 per cent of the sample had a formative window overlapping that period. Michoacán was *more* violent before the drug war (27.3) than during it (20.7), the most violent states of the early 1990s were a different set, and the two periods correlate at only 0.487. **The pre-surge period is not a clean control.** A placebo dose built from 1990-to-1997 violence gives 0.0197 (0.0259): insignificant, but larger than the surge dose itself. Appendix F gives the series and its construction.

Table 9 is the most instructive result in the paper. Replacing the four-crime index with INEGI homicide levels moves the slope coefficient from 0.016 to 0.001 and the effect at the 25th percentile from −0.81 to −0.34, and the pre-trend test still passes. Defining treatment as the *increase* over 1990-1997 rather than the level — the better-identified object in principle, because it separates the surge from states that were always violent — gives the largest and most precise estimate in the whole paper: −2.26 rank points at the 25th percentile, standard error 1.22, permutation p-value 0.077, above the United States benchmark.

And it fails the pre-trend test outright, with F = 4.66 and p = 0.005. We arrived at that specification after seeing the others, and the design's own validation rejects it. ## 6 Discussion and policy implications

Our results sit beside, rather than against, the literature summarised in Section 2.1. That literature finds that violence lowered schooling and test scores, and we do not contradict it; our own first-stage estimate for schooling is consistent with effects up to 0.42 years, which comfortably contains the magnitudes reported there. What we add is that over the range this design can detect, the same exposure did not visibly change how strongly destination depends on origin. The distinction is substantive. A shock can be costly in levels and still be distributionally neutral, and the policy implications differ accordingly.

Against the direct precedent the reading is different again. Sharkey and Torrats-Espinosa (2017) find a clear negative effect on absolute upward mobility in United States counties, and our Mexican point estimate at the same percentile has the same sign and roughly half the magnitude. A reader could take that as weak corroboration, and we think that is the most defensible reading of the state design taken alone. But the within-state municipal design, which is more precise, puts the estimate on the other side of zero and excludes their magnitude, so the two designs do not point the same way about the level even though they agree about the slope. We decline to resolve that with a preferred specification. The responsible conclusion is that Mexican data of this kind cannot yet distinguish a United States-sized effect from no effect, and that saying so is more useful than picking whichever design gives the more quotable number.

For the agenda of the Centro de Estudios Espinosa Yglesias, which frames mobility as a question of regional equality of opportunity, the finding has a specific use. The regional gradient in mobility that Delajara et al. (2022) document is large and it is not, on this evidence, a product of the violence of the past two decades. It predates the surge, it survives in states the surge barely touched, and the geography of violence is not the geography of immobility. Policy aimed at regional equality of opportunity should therefore not expect the pacification of a state to deliver mobility gains by itself.

Three limitations bound all of this. The first is the one we named in Section 4.6: the 2008-09 recession fell inside the exposure window and fell hardest on the northern manufacturing states, which are also where violence rose. State and birth-year fixed effects absorb its permanent and its national components but not a state-specific recession shock coinciding with the surge. We note there that the pandemic and the programme terminations, often raised in this context, are not confounds here because every formative window in the sample closes by 2016. The second is survivorship. The third is the mis-dating documented in Section 5.4, which attenuates the estimate by an amount we have characterised but not corrected; re-dating the dose with each cluster's own calendar is the most promising next step and requires no new data.

A methodological limitation also deserves statement. The unsupervised component of this paper validates an assumption and supplies a partition; it does not predict anything, and we have not claimed that it does. A natural extension, which we did not pursue here, would estimate heterogeneity in β₃ with an honest-sample-splitting forest in the manner of Lechner and Okasa (2025), which would replace our pre-specified subsamples with a data-driven partition while preserving valid inference. We regard that as the appropriate way to add a supervised component to this design, and we note that it would be a complement to the clusters rather than a substitute for them, since the clusters are built from the treatment's own time path and not from covariates of the respondents.

## 7 Conclusions

We have asked whether exposure to organized-crime violence between the ages of 10 and 18 raised the intergenerational persistence of socioeconomic status in Mexico, and we have answered with a bound rather than with an effect. Using a triple difference that requires the effect to appear across states and across cohorts simultaneously, and that allows persistence to differ both by state and by cohort, we estimate a change in the rank-rank slope of 0.016 per standard deviation of state violence intensity, with a standard error of 0.026 and a permutation p-value of 0.62. The identifying assumption passes the pre-trend test available for it. Within-state municipal variation tightens the bound to 8 per cent of the base slope.

Read against the one direct precedent, the bound acquires a referent. Expressed as Sharkey and Torrats-Espinosa (2017) express it — the effect on the expected destination rank of a child from the 25th percentile — our state estimate is -0.81 rank points against their -1.57, the same sign and about half the size, with an interval that contains their value. The within-state design excludes it. Mexican retrospective survey data with 31 treated states can bound an effect of that magnitude but cannot adjudicate it, and the constraint is the number of treated units rather than the number of respondents.

Four by-products are worth carrying forward. The apparent cross-cohort rise in persistence that motivates the question is a generational trend that pre-dates the surge, and a specification that does not absorb it will report a significant effect that is not there. Municipal homicide counts measure city size as much as violence, and a design that uses them without a population denominator will find a spurious negative. Unsupervised clustering of the treatment's own time path is a cheap and informative diagnostic for a parametric exposure measure: here it identified eleven states whose violence arrived a decade after the date our dose assigns them. And correcting that mis-dating, which we do, roughly doubles the point estimate while inflating its standard error more, so the correction widens the bound rather than sharpening the finding — a result we report because the opposite was our prior.

What the data cannot tell us is whether an effect smaller than 8 per cent of the base slope exists, or whether the United States magnitude holds here. Three things would move that. Rebuilding the exposure index on INEGI vital-statistics homicide records, which count deaths rather than prosecutors' files and begin in 1990 rather than 1997, would both reduce measurement error and extend the pre-period by seven years. Controlling for state economic activity interacted with cohort would address the 2008-09 recession, the one confound with the right timing. And the next ESRU-EMOVI round will supply more fully exposed cohorts. Until then, the defensible claim is the bound and the comparison, not an effect.
